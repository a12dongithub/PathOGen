\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Hematoxylin-and-eosin (H\&E) models increasingly support tumor classification, biomarker prediction, survival modeling, and general-purpose representation learning~\cite{shmatko2022ai,lu2024conch,chen2024uni}. Yet the evidence used by these systems is difficult to isolate. A prediction can change because of stain intensity, scanner characteristics, nuclear geometry, cell composition, or the organization of tumor and immune cells; site-specific histology signatures can measurably bias model accuracy~\cite{howard2021sitespecific}. Conventional explanation maps indicate where a model attends, but localization alone does not establish which biological property within the highlighted region caused the response~\cite{klauschen2024xai,zigutyte2026mopadi}.

The missing ingredient is a plausible intervention. Occluding a region, blurring it, or adding independent noise creates an input that does not resemble tissue. Generic inpainting may restore texture while silently changing cell populations. Color augmentation probes stain but cannot independently modify nuclear morphology or tumor--immune organization. Retrieval remains on-manifold but rarely produces matched samples differing in one requested property. Diffusion counterfactuals offer a route to realistic, model-relevant edits~\cite{augustin2022dvce,zigutyte2026mopadi}, but a large downstream response can still reflect sensitivity to an artifact rather than dependence on the intended evidence.

\method{} addresses this problem with condition-matched counterfactual synthesis. Each generated patch is specified by a dense cellular map and a global morphology/appearance vector. The map controls the location and density of five cellular compartments using dense diffusion conditioning~\cite{zhang2023controlnet}. Feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) controls summary statistics of nuclear geometry, texture, and RGB appearance~\cite{perez2018film}. For a counterfactual pair, the initial noise and all non-intervened conditions are held fixed. The method does not claim pixel-identical reconstruction of an observed patient patch; instead, it generates paired samples for which the requested properties are explicit and recoverable.

Controllability cannot be inferred from visual quality alone. A generator may obtain a favorable distributional score while ignoring its spatial map, or it may reproduce cell counts while yielding unrealistic stain. We consequently separate evaluation into three questions: (i) are generated patches distributionally realistic, (ii) are requested spatial and morphological conditions measurable in the output, and (iii) can accepted interventions support fair comparisons of downstream model sensitivity? The first two are evaluated here with 2,000 realism samples and 1,000-condition fidelity cohorts. The third defines a fixed protocol for pathology encoders and task-trained models without using their predictions during sample selection.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a conditional latent-diffusion architecture combining dense cell maps with blockwise FiLM controls for $512\!\times\!512$ H\&E generation;
    \item a same-noise intervention protocol that separates stain, morphology, and cellular organization while preserving the remaining conditions;
    \item a seed-selection mechanism based only on generated-versus-requested cellular agreement, together with explicit unfiltered and selected realism results; and
    \item a multi-analyzer fidelity study using \cellvit{}, \hovernet{}, and \stardist{}~\cite{horst2026cellvitpp,graham2019hovernet,schmidt2018stardist}, including a random-real-tile spatial control and within-image dose-response tests.
\end{itemize}

The distinction between selection and evaluation is central. \cellvit{} scores candidate spatial adherence, but independent \hovernet{} and \stardist{} measurements test whether the resulting gains transfer beyond the in-loop analyzer. This design turns seed selection from an informal visual choice into an auditable stage with a fixed candidate budget.
